How to grow microgreens in an apartment: your first tray

Pick an edible crop, size the tray, and follow one published method. A lamp is optional until the window falls short.

By Ryan Moalemi. Updated November 10, 2026.

Steps

Before sowing: Calculate the seed amount, then weigh your seed dry and check whether your crop needs soaking. Follow its preparation instructions, then drain soaked seed before sowing.

  1. 1

    Clean the tray

    Wash the tray and follow the sanitizer label when reusing it, including any rinse; never mix cleaning products.

  2. 2

    Moisten the medium

    Add moist soilless mix about 1 to 1.5 inches deep, or use the mat specified by your growing method.

  3. 3

    Sow evenly

    Spread the prepared seed over the moist surface and press it into contact, using the amount weighed dry before any soak.

  4. 4

    Cover if directed

    Use a cover for the germination period only if your crop's instructions call for it, and vent it if the surface stays dripping.

  5. 5

    Weight only if needed

    Add weight only when your crop's method specifies it, then remove it at the stage that method gives.

  6. 6

    Move into light

    Uncover into light; Maryland suggests about 16 hours a day, with a lamp if the window falls short.

  7. 7

    Water and drain

    Water from below when the medium needs it, then remove the growing tray from the reservoir so excess water can drain.

  8. 8

    Harvest above the mix

    At your crop's harvest stage, cut above the mix, leave the roots behind, and wash the greens before eating.

Your daily check

Check the tray each day. Water according to the medium, crop, and kit instructions; a fixed weekly houseplant schedule is not a useful assumption for every microgreen tray.

  • Moisture. Check the growing medium and any reservoir. Add water when needed for your method, rather than watering automatically by the calendar.
  • Drainage. Make sure excess water has somewhere to go. Follow the bottom-watering method for a drained tray.
  • Light and cover. Follow the crop's germination instructions, then provide light. If growth is weak or leaning, check the window and lighting guide.
  • Condition. Look for unexpected growth or damage. If you suspect mold, use the guidance below; changing airflow does not make a spoiled crop safe.

Keep one useful record: crop, dry seed weight, sowing date, harvest date, cut weight, and anything that went wrong. Use the printable checklist. A record from your own space is more useful for planning repeat trays than assuming a kit's photograph is a yield promise.

Sprouts, mold, and what not to eat

Microgreens are cut above the mix. Sprouts are eaten whole, root and seed included, after a warm wet germination. The FDA says raw sprouts are a higher-risk food, including sprouts grown at home. Microgreens are still raw produce: wash hands, tools, and the cut greens. Washing reduces bacteria and does not eliminate them. Do not use soap on the greens.

If the crop has mold, discard it and clean and sanitize reusable trays before starting again. Changing the watering or airflow does not make a moldy crop suitable to eat. LSU AgCenter says any crop that grows mold should be discarded and the trays sanitized, and that root hairs are commonly mistaken for mold. Unusual growth that moves up the stems is mold. If you are unsure, discard the crop.

Illinois Extension says not to grow tomato, eggplant, or pepper as microgreens because of toxins in the stems and foliage. Keep those transplant seedlings off the plate. The transplant method is the seed-starting guide.

FAQ

How big is the reference tray?

About 10 by 20 inches in the Utah State and Maryland guides. A smaller tray uses the same steps and less seed.

Do I need fertilizer?

Maryland says no. Penn State says little or none, and that 50 to 100 ppm nitrogen is generally enough if you do feed.

Can I use only a window?

Often, for a short crop. Maryland says microgreens generally do better with about 16 hours of light. Add a lamp if stems lean or stay pale.

Are these the same as sprouts?

No. You cut above the mix. The FDA's raw-sprout caution is about sprouts. Wash microgreens anyway.

Will the same tray keep producing after I cut it?

For budgeting, plan a fresh sowing for each harvest rather than assuming repeated cuts. Leave the roots behind when harvesting, then follow the cleaning and growing-medium instructions for your next batch. If you experiment with regrowth, record it separately instead of counting it as a guaranteed yield.

Can I start with both tiny seeds and sunflower?

Use separate trays and the instructions for each crop. Small seeds and large seeds can need different sowing weights and preparation. For example, Utah State lists no presoak for broccoli and radish, and a presoak for sunflower. The calculator shows the published starting rate and preparation notes for the selected crop.

Evidence notes

Seed preparation. Utah State lists about 6 hours of soaking for pea and 12 hours for sunflower. Weigh seed dry before any soak, drain before sowing, and skip soaking for crops whose instructions do not call for it.

Covering and weight depend on the crop and method. Penn State describes weighting pea and sunflower. Utah State uses about 2 to 5 pounds on the covering tray for the first half of the dark period, then removes the weight as roots establish. These details do not make weight a required step for every crop. Maryland suggests about 16 hours of light; Utah State's lamp schedule is 18 hours on. Follow one crop-specific method rather than combining parts of different schedules.

Harvest timing is not one number. Maryland: about 2 to 3 inches and 7 to 21 days. Penn State: about 1.5 to 3 inches and 7 to 21 days. Utah State lists crop-by-crop windows, such as radish about 8 to 12 days and pea about 8 to 10. Storage guidance also differs: Maryland up to 10 days refrigerated, Iowa State up to a week, a University of Vermont handout about 2 to 3 days. Penn State cites dark storage near 41°F for about 10 to 14 days versus about 2 to 4 days at room temperature. Cut what you will eat soon.

Illinois splits faster crops (radish, kale, broccoli, mustard) from slower ones (pea, sunflower, basil, cilantro, beet). Do not mix species in one tray. They finish on different days.
