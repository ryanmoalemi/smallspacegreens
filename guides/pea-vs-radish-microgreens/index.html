Pea shoots vs radish microgreens: which should you grow?

Radish is spicy and lighter on the scale. Pea is milder, soaked, and a much heavier sowing.

By Ryan Moalemi. Updated November 9, 2026.

Radish microgreens are a spicy, smaller-seeded crop sown at 42 grams per 10 by 20 inch tray in the Utah State University Extension table. Pea shoots are a milder, large-seeded crop sown at 150 grams on that same tray, after a presoak. Both are common first crops. They are not substitutes for each other in the scale, the jar, or the sandwich. This comparison uses extension figures for seed weight, soak, days, and the flavor words those publications already use. It is not a taste test run for this site.

Choose radish for a spicy green, no presoak in the Utah State table, and 42 grams on a 10 by 20 inch tray. Choose pea for a milder shoot, a soak, and 150 grams on that same tray. Pea does not automatically cost more per tray than every radish. Your seed price and the grams decide that.

The day counts do not line up perfectly, and they should not be forced to. Utah State separates the crops by a few days and by a longer cover for pea. Mississippi State puts both in a 7 to 10 day group. Illinois flags pea as slower to germinate even though the eating stage can still arrive quickly once it is up. Plan a radish tray as a fast brassica and a pea tray as a soaked, weighted, heavier sowing that may need a little more patience at the start. Check the tray instead of the calendar alone. Harvest, in Utah State's words for microgreens generally, can begin when the first true leaves show, or sooner if you prefer that stage.

Seed, soak, and the scale

Forty-two grams of radish against 150 grams of pea is the whole cost and storage story. On a half-size 10 by 10 inch tray the calculator's area rule cuts both in half: about 21 grams of radish and about 75 grams of pea. You can see the method in the density guide. Mississippi State's volume example for radish on a 10 by 20 inch tray is about 2 to 3 tablespoons. It does not give a matching tablespoon line for pea in the passage that states the radish amount. Weigh pea. A spoon of dry pea is a poor guess next to a spoon of radish, because the seeds are not the same size.

Soak pea, not radish, if you follow Utah State. Six hours is that table's pea soak. University of Maryland Extension puts pea in a broader 6 to 12 hour group that also includes sunflower, beet, and others, and does not put radish on that soak list. Francesco Di Gioia shows pea after roughly 10 hours in one photo sequence and describes an 8 to 12 hour soak for larger seed, with rinses so the seed gets oxygen. Mississippi State says gray sugar peas can germinate in waves over 5 or 6 days, and that an overnight soak evens them out. Any of those soaks is a published way to hydrate pea. None of them is a radish step in these sources.

After the soak, pea seed is swollen. The gram target was the dry weight. Drain the seed, then spread it. Di Gioia recommends a weight on pea during germination so the seeds stay against the mix. Utah State's general method uses about 2 to 5 pounds on the covering tray for the first half of the dark period, for the crops it is weighting, so roots grow downward. Radish, with a 1 to 2 day cover and no presoak in the Utah State table, does not need that same handling in the sources cited here. You still press radish onto moist mix so it does not sit dry on top. Maryland's press with a hand or a wood block is the shared step.

Flavor, texture, and how to use them

Utah State describes its radish microgreens as nutty and spicy, and its pea as sweet pea. Maryland says most microgreen flavor is in the leaves rather than the stems, and that stems still help you harvest and add crunch. Radish is in the cabbage family, which Maryland groups with kale, mustard, and arugula as crops that germinate and grow faster than beet-family greens. Pea is a legume shoot, not a brassica. The "heat" people expect from a radish slice is the trait the microgreen is grown for. Pea is the mild tray.

That is as far as the flavor claim should go. This site has not plated them. If you want heat, sow radish. If you want a thicker, milder shoot, sow pea. If you want both, sow two trays. Maryland, Iowa State, and Penn State advise against mixing species in one tray because they do not finish together. A pea seed still swelling on day 2 will not share a harvest day cleanly with a radish that germinates fast. Cut them separately and combine them in the bowl.

Culinary use in the Maryland guide is broad and applies to both: sandwiches, eggs, grain dishes, and a handful on a salad. Pea shoots are sturdy enough to stand in a warm dish for a moment. Radish microgreens are smaller and easier to wilt with heat, so they usually go on at the end. That is kitchen practice inferred from the size difference in the growing guides, not a timed cooking trial.

Light, water, and problems that hit one crop more

Both use a drained tray, soilless mix, and bottom water, as in the apartment guide. Radish is a fair bright-window crop. Pea still wants the soak and the weight. Do not leave the heavier pea mat sitting in a full reservoir. Penn State says to remove trays once the mix is wet. Iowa State warns that extra water leads to root rots and damping-off. If stems lean, use the windowsill guide.

Sunflower is the other large seed people pair with pea. It is not radish. Utah State lists sunflower at 48 grams, with a 12 hour soak, which is less weight than pea and more handling than radish. If pea seed is hard to store in a small kitchen, sunflower is the smaller bag of the two large-seed crops in that table. The cost side of a 150 gram sowing is in the cost guide: in Utah State's January 8, 2026 snapshot, pea was inexpensive per pound and still more than a dollar of seed per 1020 tray.

Which to sow this week

Sow radish if you want a spicy tray, a lighter seed purchase, and no soak. Sow pea if you want a sweet shoot and you are willing to soak, weigh a heavier ration, and set a weight on the tray for part of germination. Sow radish first if you are new to the method and the scale is unfamiliar. Forty-two grams is a smaller mistake than 150 grams if the tray molds. Move to pea when the watering habit is steady.

Neither crop is a sprout in the extension definition. You cut above the mix. You do not eat the pea seed hulls as the crop, though some hulls cling and can be rinsed off. The FDA's sprout warning, summarized in the apartment guide, is about sprouts grown and eaten whole. Wash the cut greens anyway.

FAQ

Which is faster, pea or radish?

It depends which clock you use. Utah State lists pea at about 8 to 10 days and radish at about 8 to 12 days, with a longer covered period for pea. Mississippi State puts both in a 7 to 10 day group. Illinois lists radish among faster crops and pea among slower seeds to germinate. Check the tray.

Do I soak radish seed?

Not according to the Utah State table, which marks radish as no presoak. Pea in that table is a 6 hour soak. Maryland's 6 to 12 hour soak list names pea and does not name radish.

Can I grow them in one tray to save space?

Extension guides say not to mix species that finish on different schedules. Use two smaller trays instead of one mixed tray. A 5 by 5 inch tray takes one eighth of the 10 by 20 inch seed rate if you scale by area.

Why does pea cost more per tray if the seed looks cheap?

Because you sow far more of it. Utah State's snapshot of January 8, 2026, shows a low price per pound for pea and a per-tray seed cost above a dollar at 150 grams. Radish used fewer grams. Your current price may differ. The calculator can apply the price on your bag.
