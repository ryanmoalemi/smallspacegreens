The drained tray sits inside the solid tray. Add water to the solid tray so it can move up through the holes, then pour out what is left once the mix is moist. Penn State says this keeps leaves and stems drier than a pour from above, which helps avoid disease.

Steps

Published waits differ. Methods and media are not the same, so the published waits are not one timer and are not two steps in a row. Utah State's coir method for a 1020 tray adds 2 cups of water to the solid tray, then discards any leftover after 30 minutes. A Cornell seed-starting handout, written for vegetable transplants, says to set containers in an inch of water for no more than an hour. Use the wait that matches your medium as a guide, then drain when the check in the steps says the mix is wet.

  1. Fill the drained tray with moist soilless mix about 1 to 1.5 inches deep, or with pre-moistened coir. Level it. Maryland and Illinois both use a 1 to 1.5 inch fill for moist soilless mix. Utah State pre-moistens coir to the feel of a wrung-out sponge.
  2. Set the growing tray in the solid tray. Add a shallow layer of water to the solid tray. Penn State describes a home grower placing trays in a tub of shallow water.
  3. Check the mix before you drain. Look for a darker, wetted surface, like the mix in the photo, without a puddle left under the roots. Penn State says to take the tray out once the potting soil or medium is wet enough, then let the extra water drain. If the mix is still dry, keep waiting, up to the published wait for your method.
  4. Lift the growing tray out and empty the solid tray. Do not leave roots sitting in a puddle. Illinois says not to let water puddle in the bottom of trays.
  5. Mist only while new seed still needs a wet surface. After roots hold the seed, water from the bottom when the tray feels light. Illinois says to test moisture by lifting the tray: if it is light, it is too dry.

If the crop molds

If the crop has mold, discard it and clean and sanitize reusable trays before starting again. Changing the watering or airflow does not make a moldy crop suitable to eat. LSU AgCenter says any crop that grows mold should be discarded and the trays sanitized. Root hairs are commonly mistaken for mold. Unusual growth that migrates up the stems is mold. If you are unsure, discard the crop.

For the next sowing, empty the reservoir sooner and use fresh mix if the last tray rotted. Maryland allows a half-and-half refresh only after old roots have dried. Iowa State ties root rots and damping-off to overwatering.

Rocks in the bottom of a pot are not drainage. UC sources say a hole is what lets water leave. On a windowsill, empty the solid tray in the sink.

FAQ

How long should a tray sit in water?

Until the mix is moist, then out. That check is the signal, not a second soak. Utah State's coir method waits 30 minutes. A Cornell seed-starting handout says no more than an hour in an inch of water. Use the wait that matches your medium, and pick the short end if the mix is already dark and wet.

Can I bottom water a tray with no holes?

No. Water has to enter through holes, and extra water has to leave. The solid tray is only the reservoir.

Should I add gravel?

No. The UC container handout says rocks do not replace a drainage hole.

Is misting the same thing?

No. Mist the surface for the first days. Bottom water after the mix can wick, so the leaves stay drier.

Evidence notes

Penn State says sub-irrigation keeps leaves and stems drier, which matters for disease, and tells home growers to remove the tray once the potting soil has been wetted. Di Gioia says to wet the medium from below until it is wet enough, then let the extra water drain, and later to water from the bottom only if necessary to avoid excess moisture and mold. Maryland says mist first, then water from the bottom or the top, and keep the media moist but not saturated. Illinois says to water from the bottom after germination, to check moisture by lifting the tray, and not to let water puddle in the bottom of trays. Mississippi State says bottom watering is effective on a kitchen windowsill and warns not to overwater or dislodge germinating seeds from a mat.
