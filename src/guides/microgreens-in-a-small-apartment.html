First tray. One edible crop, a shallow drained tray, soilless mix about 1 to 1.5 inches deep, and light after the seed is up. University of Maryland Extension describes harvest around 2 to 3 inches, often 7 to 21 days after sowing. Do not eat tomato, pepper, or eggplant seedlings.

Calculate the seed or compare a kit with DIY trays.

What you need

  • Tray. A drained tray nested in a solid one. A clean food container with holes works.
  • Mix. Soilless mix or coir, not garden soil. Maryland says no fertilizer is required.
  • Seed. Untreated seed you will eat as a seedling. Weigh it.
  • Water. Mist at the start, then water from below.
  • Light. A bright window or a lamp after germination. A fan helps if the air is still.

Steps

  1. Wash the tray. If you reuse it, follow a sanitizer labeled for that surface and the cited extension handout. Do not treat a shorthand percentage as a finished recipe, and do not mix cleaning products. Rinse when the label says to rinse.
  2. Fill with moist soilless mix about 1 to 1.5 inches deep, or set a mat in the tray. Francesco Di Gioia at Penn State wets the mix from below before sowing.
  3. Weigh dry seed for your tray with the calculator. Spread to the edges first, then the middle. Press the seed onto the mix.
  4. Soak pea about 6 hours and sunflower about 12 hours if you follow Utah State. Maryland lists a 6 to 12 hour soak for several large or hard seeds. Weigh them dry, then soak.
  5. Cover and keep the tray dark until it is up. Utah State uses about 2 to 5 pounds on larger seed for part of that time so roots grow down. Vent the cover if the surface stays dripping.
  6. Uncover into light. Maryland suggests about 16 hours. Utah State's lamp schedule is 18 hours on. A south window can be enough. See the windowsill guide if stems lean.
  7. Water from the bottom, then take the tray out of the reservoir. The bottom-watering guide has the diagram.
  8. Cut above the mix when true leaves open, or a little sooner. Leave the roots. Wash before you eat.

Sprouts, mold, and what not to eat

Microgreens are cut above the mix. Sprouts are eaten whole, root and seed included, after a warm wet germination. The FDA says raw sprouts are a higher-risk food, including sprouts grown at home. Microgreens are still raw produce: wash hands, tools, and the cut greens. Washing reduces bacteria and does not eliminate them. Do not use soap on the greens.

If the crop has mold, discard it and clean and sanitize reusable trays before starting again. Changing the watering or airflow does not make a moldy crop suitable to eat. LSU AgCenter says any crop that grows mold should be discarded and the trays sanitized, and that root hairs are commonly mistaken for mold. Unusual growth that moves up the stems is mold. If you are unsure, discard the crop. This site has no diagnostic photo.

Illinois Extension says not to grow tomato, eggplant, or pepper as microgreens because of toxins in the stems and foliage. Keep those transplant seedlings off the plate. The transplant method is the seed-starting guide.

FAQ

How big is the reference tray?

About 10 by 20 inches in the Utah State and Maryland guides. A smaller tray uses the same steps and less seed.

Do I need fertilizer?

Maryland says no. Penn State says little or none, and that 50 to 100 ppm nitrogen is generally enough if you do feed.

Can I use only a window?

Often, for a short crop. Maryland says microgreens generally do better with about 16 hours of light. Add a lamp if stems lean or stay pale.

Are these the same as sprouts?

No. You cut above the mix. The FDA's raw-sprout caution is about sprouts. Wash microgreens anyway.

Evidence notes

Harvest timing is not one number. Maryland: about 2 to 3 inches and 7 to 21 days. Penn State: about 1.5 to 3 inches and 7 to 21 days. Utah State lists crop-by-crop windows, such as radish about 8 to 12 days and pea about 8 to 10. Storage guidance also differs: Maryland up to 10 days refrigerated, Iowa State up to a week, a University of Vermont handout about 2 to 3 days. Penn State cites dark storage near 41°F for about 10 to 14 days versus about 2 to 4 days at room temperature. Cut what you will eat soon.

Illinois splits faster crops (radish, kale, broccoli, mustard) from slower ones (pea, sunflower, basil, cilantro, beet). Do not mix species in one tray. They finish on different days.
